## Abstract

Twentieth-century physics rests on two pillars: general relativity (continuous spacetime) and quantum mechanics (discrete yet probabilistic). These frameworks remain incomplete and mutually inconsistent, leaving unresolved issues including singularities, renormalization, unidentified dark matter, and the cosmological constant problem.

Harmonic Lattice Theory posits that spacetime is realized as a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystalline lattice whose nodes are topologically equivalent to C60 fullerenes. Position and momentum are constrained by topological symmetries of the E8 root system and a natural ultraviolet cutoff length $L_{\mathrm{btp}}$, rather than by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle as a fundamental law. Within this framework, gravity emerges as an elastic restoring force of the lattice, dark matter is identified with topological solitons localized in octahedral and tetrahedral voids, and the domain of validity of continuum field equations is sharply delineated.

The theory is characterized by four primary numerical constants: 24 (topological vortex factor), 60 (electron soliton multiplicity), 80 (E8 tensor rank), and 0.25948 (FCC void packing fraction). Explicit predictions are offered for the dark-matter particle mass (12.68 TeV), spin-independent cross section ($2.4 \times 10^{-46}\,\mathrm{cm}^2$), and spectral features that can be tested by existing and forthcoming experimental programs.
